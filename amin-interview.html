Haron Amin: A Spiritual Journey

Oral History Essay · 2012

This interview was a doctoral program assignment. Haron Amin reviewed the final text, and a copy was shared with a representative of his family after his passing. If you are a family member or representative of his estate and have questions or concerns, please contact Annabel Lille Hertz.

Introduction

As a country that was geographically caught in the “Great Game”—the expansionism of Tsarist Russia into Muslim Central Asia in the 1800s and the British Empire’s response, including protection of its Indian colony—Afghanistan has historically been destabilized from both the east and west.1 Beginning with the first British incursion, both empires tried to install regimes in Afghanistan, eventually agreeing (without Afghan input) on borders that made Afghanistan a buffer state. This lasted until the 1917 Bolshevik Revolution, when each of the two great powers attempted to buy off the Afghans with aid and military training and equipment—a pattern that continued during the Cold War.2

After the 1973 leftist coup that overthrew the Afghan King, the 1978 Saur (socialist) Revolution, and the subsequent Russian invasion of 1979 that killed a million Afghans and was (ironically) aimed at installing a less extreme socialist government as a bulwark against the anti-Soviet mujahedeen (which were partly funded by the US), the country never returned to self-sufficiency, stability, or self-governance.3 As a result of the Soviet withdrawal a decade later, the infighting and anarchy that ensued, and war with the US following September 11, Afghanistan continues to suffer. Moreover, the planned drawdown of US forces in 2014 poses a new round of challenges for the Afghan economy.

The US government has responded to this in part by promoting a “New Silk Road Initiative” to help develop Afghanistan as a trade and energy corridor in the region. In 2011, out of personal and professional interest, I followed this initiative through an informal policy network dedicated to maintaining US-Afghan relations. At one of the meetings, I met Haron Amin, a former Afghan diplomat whose family had fled Kabul after the Soviet invasion and had landed in Lahore, and then Frankfurt, before finally settling in Los Angeles.

I learned that Haron later returned to Afghanistan twice to serve in combat under Commander Ahmad Shah Massoud, who remains a national hero for resisting the Soviet occupation as well as the civil war that followed Soviet withdrawal—including the rise of the Taliban and Islamic fundamentalism. Massoud thus symbolizes the Afghan drive for self-determination and a more moderate and responsible government that has yet to be realized.

I contacted Haron to request an oral history interview to learn about life in pre-Soviet and revolutionary Afghanistan, in exile, and in combat with Massoud, and the impact of these experiences on him personally. Haron and I spoke remotely on October 16, 2012, and I asked him: what was it like to live through the Soviet invasion, and in exile, and to return to fight for your country?

The 1978 Saur Revolution and the 1979 Soviet Invasion

Born in Kabul to a well-established family that owned the largest automotive and manufacturing enterprise in Afghanistan, Haron had been living happily in an affluent suburb of Kabul (reflecting the growth of the Afghan middle class in the 1960s) when the coup occurred. Until that day, nine-year-old Haron’s life had consisted of classes, soccer, the evening meal, and after-dinner discussions.

“We were at school, and there was a major shelling that happened outside. We’d never heard anything like that.” While both of Haron’s grandfathers occasionally hunted, Haron had never heard “guns roaring that deep and that loud.” Pandemonium erupted and the students were told to assemble on the first floor. Haron’s geography teacher, now carrying a Kalashnikov—one of the most shocking sights of Haron’s life—nervously instructed his class that “There will be a change in Afghanistan and it will be good for all of you.” For Haron:

The teacher’s words reflected a sense of vengeance from someone who could barely make ends meet, coming from a background of deprivation. It was one of the things that was a stark contrast between what I had and someone like him. It also led to two of three of my best friends disappearing…one by one. To this day I don’t know if [two of them] them were killed.

Haron and his classmates were terrified and confused, especially by the echo of guns shooting into buildings and the sound of buildings collapsing. A driver came to school to bring Haron home. The coup went from morning to late afternoon and involved jets flying over Kabul and bombing the city. By evening, normal TV programming had turned into long speeches.

For the first time I heard the word proletariat and the bourgeois…I couldn’t understand why my father would say ‘these are communists’ and I couldn’t really relate how—because I went to a French school—bourgeois and ‘communism’ went together. It was like ‘something here is not right.’ But it was too much, it was really too much. And all I could think about was ‘what’s going to happen to my best friends? Am I going to be able to see them again?’ At that age all you want to do is go back to class.

Soon after the coup, everything normalized. There was a change in the government, and then a change of the colors of the Afghan flag—and the emblem of the flag, depending on who was taking over. But the situation quickly deteriorated:

The marches got very long. ‘Long live this and down with this,’ and ‘Death to America, death to capitalism, long live the Soviet Union, long live the leader.’ So, there was roll call and some ideological insertions into the curriculum. A picture of the leader as the ‘genius of the east, and a genius of the west’ was posted on the wall, but the rumor around school and the rumor around Afghanistan was that these are bad guys. Afghanistan was going down and it was not going to get better…it was one thing for people to say that, then you start seeing that your friends are disappearing. The Communists really had two methodologies— elimination or exile. It’s that simple. [People] got killed, were put in jail, or forced into exile. There were massive killings. 14,000 people in one day.

When Haron’s friends disappeared, the revolution “became very personal.” The nightly debates and humor and fun his family had enjoyed were replaced by a somber and serious mood, including adults-only, anti-communist meetings behind closed doors. The school curriculum changed. In Biology class, the theory of evolution was emphasized, as were the teachings of “the great Karl Marx.” This was a very visible change for Haron because “Afghanistan at the time was a much more advanced place than a lot of the countries in the region.” Haron attributed part of its advanced education system to the King, who had muted the effects of the Islamic fundamentalist education essentially created by the British:

Before, in the Islamic curriculum, from the 12th century on, they were teaching sciences across the Islamic world. When the British came in, they wanted to do the rest of it themselves and just have exclusive Islamic schools. The roots of the Taliban teaching or the roots of the Islamic schools did not [exist prior to the arrival of] the East India Company. The King in the 1880s made sure that the Mullahs would be limited. He could not deny Islamic studies but they were one or two topics among fourteen. It was very balanced.

However, because “the Communists were much more sophisticated than the Islamists,” they took over Afghan TV and hired the Japanese to run it and “they did a very methodic job.” The Communists thus took control of the media and the government, but the Islamists had control of the rest of the country. As a result, everyone in Kabul was gradually leaving. Haron’s brothers were 18 years old and supposed to be drafted into the army, so his parents sent them to Pakistan. Haron’s parents bribed officials so that Haron would not be forced to join the Communist youth political party. “Proletariat farmers” and the Minister of Finance harassed Haron’s grandfather—who owned farms and a factory that made vehicles and parts—for his land. During one prominent altercation with the farmers, Haron’s grandfather hit his head, got a concussion, and died.

The breaking point for Haron’s family, however, came when the Soviets invaded Afghanistan to defend the government because it was unable to hold the provinces. While there had been debates about communism and capitalism throughout the 1970s on the lawn of the university in Kabul, the citizens of Kabul had no knowledge or prediction of the Soviet invasion. Once it happened, Haron’s family knew the situation was irreversible.

Haron witnessed the invasion on TV. He saw massive Soviet tanks and planes bringing supplies to “boost morale,” deliver “development,” and crush “rogue elements.” He watched reports on the “natural deaths” of leaders (who were in reality suffocated and poisoned). Again, the flags were changing; Haron had to frequently spray paint the flags he was required to make at school with new emblems because the design kept changing. And once again, the situation took a turn for the worse:

At one of the events at the stadium in Kabul, they had painted the pigeons completely red, [the doves were painted] the color of revolution, the color of change. And then when they would say things like ‘all we need is 15,000 men left in Afghanistan’ it meant that was all they needed for a revolution…the rest was rubbish. [15,000 were needed] to breed the right people, the right generation, the right kind, the right mix…the rest are expendable… [there were] massive killings of opening fire on a demonstration.

While Haron didn’t witness mass graves, people talked about the prisons filling up by day and emptying at night. Finally, when Haron’s father was imprisoned for financially assisting the Resistance, he sent a note to Haron’s mother telling her to take the kids and leave Afghanistan. Within a month, Haron’s mother packed up everything and her children (Haron’s father would be released a short time later thanks to a buyoff) and loaded them onto a bus in the middle of the night (to their surprise) to cross the border legally, and with Afghan passports, into Pakistan. Since Haron’s uncle had already fled the draft for Lahore and bought an ice factory, it made sense for the family to go there.

They arrived at the border to see a mass of people at the gate. Everyone in Kabul had left their homes with caretakers, taking only cash with them. At the time, there was no policy or designation for Afghan refugees, whose numbers would reach an astonishing 6.2 million within the first year of the invasion.4

Life in Exile: Frankfurt (via Lahore) and Los Angeles

Haron found Lahore very hot, humid, and dirty, and the food very spicy. He was exposed to water buffalo, the game of cricket, salamanders, and cockroaches. His family was received openly, though they wore jeans instead of the traditional shalwar kameez. Also, Haron’s family was accustomed to eating much more than the Pakistanis did. As such, Haron would go to the bakery and hold up the line buying 150 pieces of bread. “They saw us as animals from the mountains who ate too much.” Haron’s family stayed in Lahore for only eight months while they completed paperwork to go to Germany, where the kids could continue with their education. It was a time of hibernation and transition, without school or friends.

Despite his exposure to German culture through his classmates, Haron found life in Germany totally different because of the downgrade in lifestyle from Kabul. There was no more domestic help. Instead of living in a big house, his family occupied four two-room apartments on a single floor, and they kept all their doors open. It was crowded—and very confusing for the Germans. Food was also a big issue and Haron’s family had “a terrible time with all the pork” and with the “German sausage breakfasts.” Rice and bread were also hurdles, as was the lack of available spices, though Haron’s mother had brought some that lasted the family six months. The family tried to adjust:

We had to find Turkish Muslims that could slaughter the meat in an Islamic way. Otherwise, My father had to go all the way to the village then basically put this animal in the back of a truck…in the back of a taxi…and the taxi was going nuts- the taxi driver…you know, a headless sheep in the back of the car. Most of the time they didn’t want to accept it because they would say, ‘I don’t want to be responsible for the murder of this animal.’

During the almost three years that Haron spent in Frankfurt, he learned calligraphy, developed a taste for fries, and began to master Farsi, German, soccer, and ping-pong. He also participated actively in demonstrations against the Soviet occupation at home (the city was a conglomerate of all the Resistance groups), increasing his understanding of what was happening with jihad and Afghanistan. He became a nationalist, and was heavily inspired by Commander Massoud, who had gained legendary status for his leadership of the Panjshir Valley offensives against the Soviets. The Resistance group always spoke about the commanders and Haron immediately noticed that Massoud had a thin beard that “made him more attractive” and that he wore army (instead of mountain) gear that made him appear “very organized.” For Haron, the legend of Massoud “flew with the image.”

In the meantime, Haron’s uncle had been a New Yorker since 1973 and had married an American, and he sponsored and hustled Haron’s family to leave Germany for New York. When Haron’s family got to America, however, they decided within two days that the living conditions were too much like Germany and that California’s weather and housing situation would be preferable. By the time they arrived in Glendale shortly thereafter, Haron had already made a connection to the Resistance office there. His first impressions of Los Angeles were memorable:

I’ll never forget the smell of the first days walking in Glendale. Because Glendale was the bedroom of Hollywood. It was absolutely fantastic those first days and weeks before school started. The smell of the streets and then going to Lucky’s and buying groceries…we had neighbors to one side who were Mexican, then behind us we had a composer… he was British…next to us was a Scottish guy and across us were Americans. It was the first time I had exposure to the accents. I always told my friend next door that his father’s accent was really twisted. I was a bit confused about how to say words.

Haron’s family represented the fourth ethnicity to arrive in the predominantly Caucasian, upper-class community; Hispanics, Armenians, and a few Iranians preceded them. Haron had to adjust to American football (rather than continue soccer) and American cafeteria food. He focused on excelling in school and devotion to a higher cause:

A sense of individuation in me took place in a big way. The fundamental thing that happened was a sense of nationalism…a vow came to me that ‘indifference in the face of injustice is injustice onto itself.’ That really took a stronger turn every day in my psyche and in my conscience. That I could not be standing indifferent and I really had to do something. It was really internal. In a way it also saved me from mainstream America…. although having a crush on this girl or that girl was happening, but this other side really just quelled a great deal of that by giving me responsibility and focus in that area. So, in a way what it did was give me the kind of focus that most people don’t have. But it really just saved me.

He started speaking English very quickly and was immediately recruited by a minister who needed translation for Afghan Resistance fighters being treated in LA hospitals. Haron obliged, often doing his homework in the hospital.

His commitments and connection to religion grew and, by high school, he’d published a newsletter called Afghanistan Jihad News. He also had tough periods with young romance, and later—after his first two years of jihad in Afghanistan—he would return to his high school football field, stand in the middle of it, and ask himself, ‘what’s the greater purpose of life?’

The Return to Afghanistan and Commander Massoud

By his own description, Haron had become a very pious young man, but with character.

College was a period of reflection for him and his parents were proud of the scholarship he’d been granted. He experienced the paradoxical influences of girlfriends who pushed him towards academic excellence, and then the pull of jihad. He chose premed, but did not want to cut open frogs, and thought, “this ain’t me.” Also, the Geneva Accords—the peace agreement between Afghanistan and Pakistan—were being signed, and the Soviets were pulling out of Afghanistan. Haron knew he had to pack up and go.

It was a matter of when you go for the ultimate sacrifice cause there’s no chance of coming back. What do you do? You really just gear up for it. It’s just like going to a Buddhist temple. You really want to do some preliminary things. That is where I was really. This was a pure war. This is what god had called for and we were preordained to engage in it. That’s where I was mentally and I was one of the cleanest guys, purest guys. It was like the attainment of nirvana if you ask me.

In the weeks before his journey to Afghanistan, Haron would drive with his sister on the Pacific Coast Highway, walk on the beach in Malibu, look at the ocean, and drive through the nearby canyons that reminded him of Afghanistan—ventures that solidified his thoughts on leaving and prepared him.

When I secretly got my passport and there was one morning when I woke up and I said, ‘I am doing this’ and my father said, ‘I am going to disown you’ and my mother said, ‘are you crazy’ and my sister was dropping to my feet saying, ‘are you crazy, don’t go, you’re going to get killed’ and all that stuff and it was very simple. I basically challenged them from a very ideological perspective and a very nationalist perspective…I said ‘if I was the only son and the only breadwinner, you’d have justification to stop me. Here you have six boys and two girls and a company and all that stuff and what the heck are you stopping me for? From a nationalist perspective isn’t a shame that out of all these men standing here, not a single one has the courage and going and facing the enemy.’

A very righteous, robust argument coming from a very untainted, untouched, voice of a youth. [My parents knew about my drive] over time…I think there was something hidden in me and, at that point, no one could stand clearly in my way, and I’d made my arguments. [I told them they] ‘could disown me and if something happens to me, be regretful, or if something happens to me, be proud. And if I come back, be even more proud’… it was very methodic but very clear arguments.

Upon leaving Glendale, he stopped in Washington, DC (with very little money) on his way to Afghanistan. There he joined Massoud’s political party, and stayed with relatives or at the office, until the office rented him a place. He lasted a few months, during which time he raised the party’s newsletter circulation from 500 to 10,000, made videos, and reached out to youth in the pre-Internet era. The closer he got to the issue, however, the more he wanted to get to the heart of the “holy struggles,” and while he enjoyed consuming gyros in DC, the rest of his stopover was spiritual. In Washington, Haron developed a love for, and knowledge of, poetry.

In March of 1988, he landed in Peshawar, where the biggest base of fighters and mujahedeen was, but he saw only refugees and corrupt leaders and, after a few weeks, continued on to Afghanistan and to Massoud.

I saw Massoud and all the feelings were absolutely right. I’d gone for a couple of months- six weeks maximum- and after three days of being with him, I said, ‘look I’ve come here with the intention of a couple of weeks, couple of months but now I’m going to translate that into a couple of years.’ He was absolutely shocked. Because as soon as I arrived in the valley, I was swimming in the river washing myself one day when my foot got injured by a tank piece that was flying into the water, floating in fact…food was meager along the way but Massoud always managed to at least put me in a place where there was at least decent staple…beans, rice and meat and stuff like that.

Haron did tough boot camp training for six weeks and was then directly under Massoud’s command “24/7” and saw “big time combat” in a number of battles during the withdrawal phase. “Massoud always took the lead on these combats himself.” Massoud’s strategy was to move in from the countryside and choke the capital and he felt he had to prepare everyone psychologically, spiritually, and physically for this, lest his army drown in the mess that Kabul had become. That Haron’s initial feeling about Massoud did not change over the years but only got stronger

tells you that the person had an enormous amount of integrity. And integrity is the key to character. [Massoud] fought some of the cleanest battles and did an enormously superb job…Massoud took war very seriously, the way an army does. If you take him out of Afghanistan the rest is just of it is just basically rag-tag army. The fact that he never had the resources to turn it into a professional army is a different story. But he had the ability to take a peasant army and turn it into a professional army.

Massoud went to the homes of the troops who died under him and asked their wives for forgiveness, and to make sure to put them on the list for rations. He never took the sole breadwinner of a house to fight.

Haron himself buried at least 60-70 people and felt grief over the losses, but regarding his own role in combat, he

had not a single remorse or wrong feeling about anything I was doing. Nothing was scaring me. I was absolutely ready to die. I was absolutely ready to be a victor. I was absolutely ready to take any venture possible. So, no sense of fear whatsoever. There would be times the guy in back of me along the path we were walking on would be blown up. Nothing would happen to me.

Within two years, Massoud saw the value of Haron’s skills with languages and asked him to go to Washington to represent the Northern Alliance. At first, Haron was belligerent, thinking, ‘who does this guy think he is,’ but then realized it was for the best. Haron thus became the PR machine for Massoud, liaising with the political and development communities.

Massoud’s vision for Afghanistan was “responsible replacement…to offer an alternative, to bring back order…nothing was more holy than having order and civility in place.” According to Haron, Massoud was “very compelling in his approach to intellectual discourse. All the books written about him talk about this. He was fiercely independent and highly focused.” Massoud was also very well read and capable of memorizing texts word for word, and recalling incredible and meticulous details of every event.

Yet despite Massoud’s brilliance, Haron faced many difficulties building support for the Commander. “Pakistan didn’t like him because he was independent, charismatic, and talented…charisma always nails you in the head. Charisma and talent always deprive you of resources, and then you get a lot of enmity and hatred.”

While Haron traveled back and forth between the US and Afghanistan, he directly reported to Massoud. He also undertook more education in California, making a regular transition “from the mountains of rice and beans down to Applebee’s….and comfort,” including the abundance of food at home and his mother’s cooking. Each time he traveled to the region, he entered through Pakistan and spent 13 days en route to Afghanistan with no water or shower. After that, having a shower in California was amazing, as was coming to the first bathhouse in Pakistan and smelling soap or lathering with shampoo over and over. This contrast between worlds was only the physical component for Haron, however, because he was on a mission. “There’s a typical Special Forces kind of mentality of a man with a mission.”

Haron also led a campaign in DC for seven months aimed at obtaining an aid package for Massoud. The package, however, was stymied by the CIA and the ISI (Pakistan’s intelligence agency). “The US simply didn’t know the region and thought ‘screw the Afghans…let the Pakistanis do it.’”

Eventually, Haron returned to combat in Afghanistan between 1995-1997 to deal with the Taliban and retrieve the bodies of the men and women who were killed in the fighting. The one thing that gave it all meaning was what he’d learned from Massoud:

‘No matter how impatient you are, no matter how difficult of a task you have, remember this, you can move mountains and it starts with a single shovel.’ He would always say things like, ‘if god hadn’t given you that feeling, and if you had not searched inside yourself for that feeling, you wouldn’t even ask that question.’ So, once you know it, then you’ve basically struck a higher consciousness than the ordinary people and you’re obligated to do it and you can do one of two things...walk away and have regret or do it and feel good about it. We always had an amazing sense of responsibility.

After 1997, Haron had to stay in the US to keep the Afghan seat at the United Nations away from the Taliban. Following the 2001 assassination of Massoud by the Taliban and the September 11 attacks, he became the official spokesperson for the Afghan government in exile. But the death of Massoud two days before September 11 was the biggest drawback of Haron’s life. The loss “negatively impacted” his relationship with God; he felt God could have prevented it. While Haron’s journey had begun before Massoud, Massoud had set a milestone for Haron to complete it:

We all engage on the path of individuation. We all seek something. And along the way we have little signals, we have little bumps, we have little falls, and we have little bleeding this and that…all of it really happens. And I think the biggest thing one can walk away with as far as being touched by Massoud is concerned…. he would always say there is no regret in reading, education and in prayer…I think the greatest achievement for mankind is not to be ordinary. Mediocrity dictates that one is just about himself…at best maybe those surrounding him. But I think supremacy of being is to put everyone else above you. So that’s when we …say that in order for a society to find salvation, it needs people who feel nothing for themselves but everything for others. And I think those are the paramount teachings of Massoud.

At the close of our interview, Haron read a passage (quoting himself) from a book about Massoud. The passage was about being transcendentally connected and Massoud being a perfect man that could connect on a deep psychic level to individuals. For Haron, life was ultimately about this search for perfection of individuals, which took a great deal of energy on many levels, and the containment of emotions. “It’s the difference between ordinary people and extraordinary people.” He felt this kind of extraordinary person was missing in today’s Afghanistan. “What was once about the cause has become to these individuals about themselves…materialism has killed their hearts.”

This sensibility is one of the reasons that after Haron served as Deputy Chief of Mission at the Afghan embassy in Washington and as Afghanistan’s Ambassador to Japan (2003), he retired from government service and now advises the public and private sectors on Afghan issues.

Concluding Thoughts

While my interview with Haron Amin gave me the opportunity to hear a firsthand account of the experience of living through the Soviet invasion, and in exile, and returning to one’s country to literally fight for its freedom, I had not anticipated the hugely important role that Commander Massoud played in Haron’s life or previously grasped the full significance of Massoud for the Afghans. Further, what was striking about Haron’s account of the development of his own religious convictions, and how religious convictions manifested in Massoud’s objectives and conduct, was a narrative not often heard in the Western media—that of devout Muslims fighting for their own liberation and against extremism. It seems the ideological debates of the 1950s-1970s among the communist, capitalist and Islamist camps were the most starkly realized in Afghanistan.
