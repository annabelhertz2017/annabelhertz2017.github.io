Drop ‘Third World’

Financial Mail, “On My Mind” · June 8, 2007

I am campaigning to remove the “Third World” from political discourse and media headlines. But this simple phrase and its siblings — the “First” and “Second” Worlds — are proving stubborn. “Third World” seemed to have disappeared from common parlance — since it was replaced by terms such as “less-industrialised nations” more than a decade ago. In fact, we spent another good decade refining the notion of “development” to reflect various aspects of human development — of which poverty is only one — to arrive at more complex analyses and deeper understanding of societies.

More recently, we have turned to “lower- or middle-income countries,” “emerging markets,” “global south,” “weak or failing states” and “post-conflict environments” to better describe economies and the political, governance, cultural and psychological challenges associated with modern nation-states.

Though also overly general and reductionist, these newer terms do not carve up the planet in a way that seemingly offers nothing but political gain in the West at the expense of the Rest (to momentarily reinforce Bill Easterly’s division). Africa is one of the key nonbeneficiaries of this lingering “ism.” The Three Worlds further belie the cross-border nature of foreign direct investment, illicit power groups, climate change or any other issue with global or security influence.

Thus when political leaders and the media use the “Third World” expression, they reinforce the ranking of cultures, the tendency to compartmentalise transnational issues and the pitting of the have-lots against the have-nots on a global basis. Take an example from the US, my home country. After hurricane Katrina, reporters exclaimed: “These are normally scenes we see in the Third World, not in America!” If their epiphany was that destruction, suffering and a lack of preparation can occur anywhere, I say welcome to the Real World! Disasters — and human miscalculation — know no tiers.

In the 2004 US presidential debates the president shrugged off the claim that he was blocking cheaper drug imports from Canada, asserting they would surely be imported because they were not coming from “some Third-World country.” The implication that Americans would never consider buying anything from the “Third World” — where products must be inferior (in fact, many “Third-World” states compete with US drug companies) — does little to advance the opening of our trade gates, to African products in particular, in a purported effort towards inclusiveness, fairness and reciprocity.

And aren’t we asking these same nations to put their young men and women on the line in war zones as peacekeepers and to maintain regional — as well as global — security?

Traditionally, the Three Worlds comprised the capitalist and hegemonic West, the communists and the underdeveloped nations. But now Africa is an investment destination for China, India and Latin America, and China is a partner of the US in the global economy and in disarmament negotiations. The list goes on.

A good reason the Three Worlds framework was dumped was because it became derogatory, and used to characterise negatively refugees and images of distant starvation and disease. The other terminologies may not be perfect, but at least they carry more nuance and less judgment.

I understand why the “Third World” is a linguistic shortcut. But it’s a shortcut precisely because it leaves almost everything out and provides no value-added insight. When we throw the Three Worlds around at high levels of political discourse and employ them to communicate important information to the public, we beg the question: what are we really conveying? I vote for all Three Worlds finally becoming passé.
