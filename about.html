About

Annabel Lille (“Lily”) Hertz’s novels, The Great Reveal (forthcoming) and Seeing Green (2012), are rooted in her experience with purpose-driven work, institutions, and the people caught between them.

When Annabel was one, her parents drove her (and their highly untrained dog) from Philly to San Francisco—not by covered wagon but rather by Rambler—so her father could practice medicine in a US Public Health Service Hospital. She grew up in the city, where her brother was born, and has also lived in DC, New York, Kingston, Brighton, Paris, Berlin, Geneva, Pompano Beach, and Boston.

Her global consciousness was catalyzed at age 14, when she saw The Last Epidemic, a film about nuclear war, though it only became a calling years later. She was inspired by Helen Caldicott, featured in that film, who herself had become an anti-nuclear activist after reading the novel On the Beach—an attestation of media’s power as a live wire, conducting values from one generation to another.

That calling led to a career advancing the public interest as an advocate, executive, and consultant in the third sector, focused mostly on improving multilateral and US foreign policy on climate change, sustainable development, and global security. Annabel’s also been an International Relations (IR) lecturer. More recently, she co-founded a social enterprise aimed at strengthening civil society, which she sees as critical to pluralism and small-d democracy.

Annabel holds an MPA from Harvard’s Kennedy School, an MA in IR from San Francisco State, and a BA in Politics from UC Santa Cruz. She completed doctoral coursework in IR at American University and was part of international fellowships and study programs at the Srebrenica-Potočari Memorial Center, the German Marshall Fund, the Rumi Forum, UNITAR/Ralph Bunche Institute, the University of Sussex, and the World Economic Forum.

About the Art

Annabel was an artist from the start, initially drawn to the graphics and colors of Maxfield Parrish and of vintage travel posters and crate labels. Her mother’s a sometime writer and artist, as was her paternal grandmother, whose father owned Lille hardware in Brooklyn and Manhattan, selling paints. By age fifteen, she was also a denizen of cafés, where she could read and write without distraction yet without being alone. They became her favorite places for conversation, salon-style gatherings, music, open mics, and studying—a lifelong love that led to her Café Culture series of paintings, exhibited in Washington, DC cafés, Art All Night (Nuit Blanche), and charity fundraisers.

On a visit to Riga, Annabel discovered that another of her series, Women of Colors, echoed the unifying, creatively colored figures of Latvian artist Daina Dagnija, who engaged with the ’60s-’70s feminist, antiwar, and environmental movements (that also shaped Annabel’s life) while in exile in the US.

Annabel still contemplates and connects in cafés and remains a coffee, and now also a matcha, aficionado.
