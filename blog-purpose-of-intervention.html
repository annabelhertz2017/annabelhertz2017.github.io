Review: The Purpose of Intervention by Martha Finnemore

August 15, 2013 · Originally posted as an Amazon review

In The Purpose of Intervention, Dr. Finnemore unpacks the discourse of military intervention by showing its various meanings and functions across time and relates these developments to historical shifts in the pattern of military interventions. By drawing on both material and ideational aspects of change and contextualizing her case studies from a multidimensional perspective, she demonstrates an effective use of a social constructivist approach to understanding the use of force in international politics: norms do in fact impact international relations.

Finnemore asks the critical question of how best to explain changes in the purpose of military intervention and the use of force between the 17th and the 21st centuries, a topic one typically expects to be analyzed from a pure power perspective. However, her case study analyses demonstrate that—contrary to more reductionist explanations of state behavior and actor motivation offered by realism and liberalism—there have been a variety of socially derived influences upon state behavior and the use of force.

More specifically, she examines how the use of force as a practice for debt collection became inappropriate and unfeasible, how intervention evolved alongside the expansion of the notion of human rights, and how social constructs of sovereignty, territory and international threats have resulted in changes to what constitutes legitimate military intervention today. In doing so, she traces the history of the underlying logic of state intervention and how notions about intervention move from being controversial or novel to “obvious.” Her documentation of the influence of norms, international law, professional epistemic communities, and NGO activists in defining the legitimacy of interventions captures the tension and cognitive dissonance that occurs as one historical construct gives way to the next and as policies, actors, and institutions both lead and follow in making corresponding adjustments.

Her examples show that state interests are indeterminate, underscoring her point that while realism recognizes shifts in interest in terms of material state power, it cannot explain shifts that occur when there is no re-distribution of this power. Rather than ignore realism’s material capabilities, she argues that legitimacy is an equally valid element of international relations. Further, by organizing her research around the question of how is change possible in both the “why” and “how” of military interventions, Finnemore demonstrates that more precise answers can be found by combining different types of analyses (in this instance, discursive, agent based, and structural) that reflect an understanding of the world as one in which material and ideational aspects are symbiotic and, at times, mutually reinforcing. Her effective use of social and institutional theory, and psychology, embedded in a foundation of International Relations (IR) theory, is innovative; her work demonstrates how IR—even in the realm of hard power issues—can benefit from more holistic approaches.

In her first case study of the process by which European nations stopped using force to collect debts in Latin America, it was the growing influence of lawyers, and the practice and profession of law overall, that constituted an “epistemic community” capable of changing the idea of what kind of behavior was appropriate for conflict resolution. At the same time, debtor states began using the language of European sovereignty against their creditor states—a highly convincing method of persuasion for legal scholars, who then later convinced their own creditor nations to sign on to an agreement for using arbitration (rather than force). Finnemore explains this change based on a rational actor analysis—it was a US State Department lawyer who spearheaded the idea within the US—combined with a sociological analysis of the emerging role of legal technocrats (compared to previous centuries’ aristocrats) and the psychological dynamics of the negotiations themselves as they pertained to agenda setting and consensus building. Together, these offer a detailed account of how decision makers changed their thinking, or were responsible for and persuaded by new norms, such that intervention patterns changed.

In this context, Finnemore makes brief mention of a larger political strategy at play at the time of the arbitration agreement but she minimizes its role as an independent variable in causing the pattern to change. Specifically, she downplays how the US, with its eye on the Panama Canal, took over debt payments of Latin American states, inserting its “dollar diplomacy” against the wishes of European bondholders. The reason this matters to Finnemore’s account is because it could detract from the rational actor and institutional factors she emphasizes and beg the question as to whether the US lawyers, even if they were acting as free agents, were merely being employed (or set loose) to make the pill of US hegemony easier to swallow for the Europeans. Under such an interpretation, arbitration would have merely served as a buffering element for the US to ensure its position of powerbroker in Latin America.

More broadly, to what extent did Manifest Destiny and the Monroe Doctrine inform the US vision of Latin America as an extension of its territory, and thus make new norms and the relinquishment of force a long-term, domestic benefit? Neocolonialism, after all, had more to do with economic control and “protection” of territory than direct subjugation of peoples. This backdrop does not necessarily weaken the role of the legal field in the acceptance of new norms but highlights the possibility that tools of persuasion and individual self interest gained momentum as a result of the US’ strategic intent to consolidate claims to the western hemisphere. Further clarification of the real limits to pan Americanism and the social costs of speculative lending, for example, might help strengthen the argument of a non-material driven shift to arbitration.

Finnemore’s second case, the evolution of humanitarian interventions based on the historical development of the extension of “humanity” to others, shows how military interventions became multilateral, focused on holding elections (rather than deposing leaders), and—since the end of the Cold War—human rights based, as evidenced by a lack of clear US interest in the Somalia and Cambodia missions (1992–1993) which were, she argues, morality based interventions.

The earlier interventions she exemplifies, such as civil society mobilization on behalf of Greeks living under Ottoman rule, show that interventions have at times been an amalgamation of geostrategic considerations and human sympathy, the latter being an emotional aspect of international relations not fully accounted for by rational models. Whereas sympathy was only extended initially to those of like races or religions through the unilateral action of the Great Powers, it nevertheless germinated the practice of protecting minorities and grew over time to the point where an intervention in Bosnia—without a similar effort in Somalia—would be perceived as racist.

As such, Finnemore’s argument that states can no longer successfully claim humanitarian justification for unilateral action is largely true. One need only recall the serious credibility problem President Bush encountered as he shifted justifications for the invasion of Iraq and tried, in the end, to argue a humanitarian case—failing to convince audiences in part because the invasion was a US led operation. On the other hand, Finnemore did not delve into the overarching structural problem of multilateral interventions as a small group of powerful states deciding on interventions in weaker states. Without a corresponding democratization of the means of deciding upon and implementing enforcement, human rights interventions by the Great Powers still retain colonial overtones. Here, Finnemore does not question whether this tension (cognitive dissonance) could be resolved through institutional change (regionally created and run peacekeeping forces perhaps) or if such changes would merely replicate the same power imbalances as well as moral social purposes.

Finnemore’s final case deals with the changing nature of “international order” as it developed across four political time spans defined as the Balance of Power, Concert of Europe, Spheres of influence, and Current System. She traces the social purpose of war from the 1700s, when it was fought for glory, to its application for commercial, territorial, dynastic, political equilibrium, and deterrence purposes. Once again, she distinguishes the concept of order from its material capabilities, noting that even the realist notion of the “distribution of capabilities” has historically been open to interpretation. Her description of the Great Powers seeking mutual security guaranteed by a league with codes uniting them against a “violator” represents not only the seeds of the League of Nations, but the transition from a European balance of power to a political equilibrium.

Finnemore shows this development was driven by the self-interest of states, but also by psychological aspects that made such negotiations possible, and by domestic factors, such as the rise of the French domestic liberal bourgeois sentiment against adventurist war. Where the religion of a state was once seen as a threat, the French Revolution made it such that dynasties feared domestic liberalism as a threat. Under the Vienna treaties, therefore, revolution was an “illegal” threat to sovereignty that caused “contagion problems,” and was therefore an appropriate target for intervention.

In terms of the Cold War (or Spheres of Influence), Finnemore highlights the tension between ideological intervention by the superpowers and the growing global norm of self-determination that resulted in covert wars and secrecy (superpowers leading a double life). Similar to the aforementioned problem of representation inherent in the Security Council composition and function, a discussion of how the grander, political implementation of this Cold War moral double standard impacted the normative function of interventions is absent. In other words, what happens to socially constructed legitimacy and social purposes when there is not only a material and ideational disconnect, but state behavior stands in direct, hypocritical opposition to stated norms?

Finally, in regard to the Current System, Finnemore identifies territory, humanitarianism, and terrorism as the present-day rationales for intervention, and that intervention is a new code word for war. She asserts that territory is no longer the marker for state success, but it’s debatable as to whether she would ascribe this quality to nations like the Kurds, who still aspire to acquire territory, or whether there exists a multi-tiered system that encapsulates established states, aspiring states, and nations with outstanding territorial grievances. Elaboration of these different situations might have nuanced her description of legitimate claims within the international system.

Moreover, in her index, she includes “weapons of mass destruction” as a 4th reason for intervention (though in the text she describes it as only “possibly emerging”) and this addition deserves more attention than it’s given. There is probably good reason for its tentative inclusion and it would be interesting to know whether Finnemore would view current attempts to link pre-emptive action in Iran to its nuclear weapons capability as legitimate or as an ongoing political struggle to define interests through discourse, and to what extent the second Iraq war thwarted any attempts at establishing legitimacy in this area.

Ultimately, Finnemore’s book leaves us off at a historical moment in which there are serious doubts that coercion is effective for changing the purpose of the intervened, and readers will have to determine the applicability of Finnemore’s framework to more recent developments. For example, the book sets out a major intervention problematic by contrasting the commitment to installing (and achieving) dramatic social and political changes in Germany and Japan following WWII, with the more limited purpose of the first Gulf War (to constrain, not replace, the Hussein regime) to indicate that regime change may be “necessary but insufficient” to bring about social changes in a state experiencing intervention—thereby creating confusion as to the social purpose of the Current System interventions she describes.

Written in 2003, The Purpose of Intervention had yet to grapple with the donor and military fatigue of Iraq and Afghanistan, or the purpose of interventions in Haiti and the Middle East, post earthquake and during the Arab Spring, respectively. In these more recent examples, by Finnemore’s words, we are witnessing a military commitment to social change in Iraq, an initial military commitment towards constraint that was tempered by nation-building and then abandoned (Afghanistan), and a previously abandoned constraining effort (peacekeeping missions to Haiti) that transitioned, post earthquake, to a committed nation-building backed by a relatively small international force of 7,000 troops. Regarding the Arab Spring, Finnemore would likely concede that the military intervention in Libya on humanitarian grounds nevertheless contained the strategic/social purpose of changing domestic society without troops on the ground or nation-building.

This all reinforces Finnemore’s point that the lines between war and intervention have blurred, but it also raises the question of how to measure and define the social purpose and legitimacy of interventions when multiple and differentiated (and possibly competing) narratives of intervention—each of which may even change its purpose throughout the duration of the intervention—are being implemented simultaneously in the international system. Perhaps the domestic fragmentation of audiences and electorates in both the states that intervene, and the states experiencing intervention, permits the situation of sustaining various legitimacies and, conversely, replicates it by continuing to influence domestic leaders’ foreign policy.

Finnemore concedes explicitly that she does not measure success of the interventions she examines. Yet if intervention in the Current System consistently fails in its stated purposes of stopping terrorism, territorial conquests, and humanitarian disasters, what are the larger implications regarding its social purpose? Extending Finnemore’s framework to address this more existentialist aspect of intervention might contribute further to the understanding of IR.

Overall, The Purpose of Intervention is a ‘must read’ for scholars and general audiences. It’s an excellent book that challenges conventional wisdom about the rationale for the use of force and demonstrates that in addition to serving stated justifications and material objectives, intervention policies also serve social purposes. Analysts can therefore build upon Finnemore’s work, and her mix of methodology, to shape inquiry into major IR issues. Her use of discourse analysis in tandem with cases that serve to identify mechanisms and processes of change provide a rich context for understanding international change, and a compelling argument for constructivism.
