Why the WEF Should Have Said No to Trump

2018

The World Economic Forum’s (WEF’s) 2018 Global Risks Report warns the world not to be complacent in the face of environmental, cyber-security, economic and geopolitical risks. Yet no other participant in the WEF’s history represents/personifies these risks more than U.S. President Donald Trump, who is not only against this year’s theme of “Creating a Shared Future in a Fractured World” but who has instead consistently taken a hammer to that world—and to women.

Piling on to this irony, Trump’s attendance means that organizations like the WEF will likely continue writing reports about gender disparities—even making inroads to address them as they have this year by having women chair the meeting—but will ultimately acquiesce to a “strong man” who exacerbates the very trends its consulted experts warn about.

The idea that by rubbing elbows with elites Trump will imbibe a multilateral consciousness or global perspective through osmosis, or through genuine engagement, has been disproven time and again. Consider Trump’s general willingness to break with—and eviscerate—liberal Hollywood elites (and his own former self) in implementing the extreme agendas of his donors and ever-shrinking political base. On the flip side, any real confrontation of Trump by other participants, or by protesters, will only feed his addiction to hostility.

True, autocrats have previously attended the Forum while sessions on democracy and human rights were discussed in the next room, but the scale and combination of the risks heightened by Trump personally—including nuclear war—are unprecedented. Indeed, the Forum is extending its speakers’ platform to an administration that is under investigation for election-related breaches in cyber-security (identified by WEF as a key risk), that is doing little to avoid a government shut down (another major risk), while implementing domestic policies that increase all the environmental risks identified in the WEF Report, i.e. biodiversity loss, extreme weather and failure to address climate change.

While the spectacle of Trump’s appearance is a great media draw, the Forum has said no before—to movie stars who diverted participant attention from problem solving, and once even to Zimbabwe dictator Robert Mugabe. As an organization with greater flexibility to choose its guests than, say, the UN, the Forum could leverage the news of rejecting Trump’s attendance to strengthen its own standards and create new expectations for the behavior of elected officials. Davos-bound CEOs who prioritize climate action and reject sexual harassment and racism in their corporations are well positioned to help the institution drive home a strong message. As WEF member, PepsiCo CEO Indra Krishnamurthy Nooyi has said about Trump: “Misogynist discourse cannot be tolerated… . [I]f we don’t nip this in the bud it is going to be a lethal force in society.” (https://fortune.com/2016/11/10/pepsico-ceo-trump-election-women/)

The sudden and dramatic disruptions the WEF Report asks readers to prepare for are already underway. Trump’s name may have not made it directly onto the WEF’s list of risks, but this would be the historical moment for the WEF to demonstrate that it is “committed to improving the state of the world” by not rewarding those whose behavior and exercise of power put the whole world at risk.
