Whale of a Ride

November 6, 2026

A tourist goes to Tromsø to see the northern lights and forget her broken heart. Instead, she takes a whale of a catamaran ride that gets her out of her head.

Amalia fell onto the twin mattress. It was only 3pm, but her body had succumbed to the darkness and cold in circadian preparation for sleep. It had only occurred to her last minute, on the flight over, that these conditions might accentuate depression. Instead, they’d drained her completely, even if only temporarily, of grief—an unexpected small mercy.

In the rush to escape her flat, torturously eerie since Cedric unexpectedly dumped her two days earlier, she’d simply targeted her bucket list without doing basic research. Hadn’t even bothered to check the forecast for Tromsø! So, she’d landed in a blizzard in her Patagonia, sufficient for New York winters but as effective as a silk robe against the whipping Arctic gusts. By the time she reached her hotel, snow flurries were pasted to her long, black hair and eyeglasses, her feet and hands were numb and her slight, 5’5” frame was shaking. And now it would be three days before she might see the northern lights!? Still, she couldn’t lie in her closet of a room watching Norwegian television. The pain would be unbearable, and Cedric would have somehow won. She should not be alone.

She got online. The next day’s group activities—feeding reindeer, ice climbing, getting dragged on a sled to a remote cabin by enslaved huskies—were sold out or cancelled. It was the single-traveler dead zone (between Xmas and the new year) and even the bars and boutiques on Tromsø’s main drag were lethargic. She scoured for another hour. Until, voilà: An eco-friendly excursion through the fjords to see Humpbacks and Orcas feeding, including expert lecture, undersea tracking technology and a lunch of fresh-caught fish. Limited to 6. “Who doesn’t love whales?” she said aloud with zero excitement. The price was obscene, but she’d already spent two months of her SOHO rent getting here, and this was a matter of mental health. She took comfort in the relatively punishing nature of the boat ride: departing the city port at 6:30am, three hours to the sighting area, three hours of moonlit “daytime” for viewing, fishing and lunch, and three hours back. She’d be suffering too much to feel her recent, shocking loss.

Despite rising early the next morning, she was late to the dock. Her globally guaranteed cell data plan wasn’t working, and she’d wandered the deserted harbor, disoriented by the darkness, looking for a sleek, hybrid electric catamaran with its lights on. When at last she found the gangway of the Rán (named for the goddess of the sea who catches sea-goers in her net), a young man swathed in a balaclava shuttled her aboard. He introduced himself as Bjørn and said the names of the four other passengers, but Amalia hadn’t paid attention because they’d already formed a couples’ clique, conversing among themselves, and she didn’t want to be triggered.

“You’re the only single,” Bjørn said to her. It was a reminder that would’ve hurt had he not said it flirtatiously, and while removing his garment to reveal an absurdly good-looking face and dark wavy hair. As he helped Amalia into a weighty, seafaring thermal jumpsuit, she checked her reflection in the galley window. She was looking extra sluggish from jet lag, melatonin and post break-up insomnia, and she regretted not having put on at least a smidge of make-up. “You look cute,” Bjørn said after zipping up her too-large outfit and snapping her life vest closed.

Everyone congregated on the deck, which smelled of bait, in their marine layers as bulky and cumbersome as space suits. Bjørn recounted the day’s agenda against a backdrop of tethered yachts and sparkling holiday lights dotting the shoreline. He gave a lesson on safely walking the gunwale between the front and rear of the boat, where to sit on oversized, waterproof pillows, and how to inflate the motorized raft used for closer-up viewing or emergencies. The Rán’s captain, Pernille of Oslo, then greeted everyone from the outdoor stern at the top of the boat. “I’m not a small talker like Bjørn,” she said. “I only engage in necessary conversation…the stereotypical Norway of being.” That broke the proverbial ice with the patrons, and they nodded and laughed.

After the tour, the couples flocked to the cabin’s main U-shaped settee around its dining table to partake of blodplätter—native Sami pancakes made with reindeer blood and served with lingonberries. They were so occupied with comparing stock purchases, exotic travel destinations and Michelin-rated restaurants that they weren’t seeing the scenery made visible by new, fluffy, glow-in-the-dark snow. Amalia stood in the galley staring at the white tops of the fjords and sipping sustainable, single-bean coffee from Uganda. Time whizzed by because of Bjørn, who was only loosely attending to the Rán’s financials and organizing the cabin. Not only was Amalia not suffering, but she was basking in Bjørn’s banter, bad jokes and occasional winks. He spoke about his youth in a village on the Nordkyn Peninsula, his family and his dream of owning his own tour company. She shared tales of her university days and current life in SoHo working in an art gallery. She even mentioned the break-up in passing and was rewarded with empathy (but not too much).

They were in a bubble and Amalia wanted it to stay that way. No one else except Orcas and Humpbacks allowed! Each was enjoying the attention of—and tension with—the other, knowing it wouldn’t go any further than the Rán. Eventually, though, he switched places with Pernille, who gave a lecture on the region’s history (she was the promised expert and a PhD) and kept Amalia’s mind off both Bjørn and Cedric. Pernille described the topography of the Sápmi, the territory of the native Sámi, and the Sámi’s nomadic herding from the plains to the coastline’s remnants of ancient, glaciated valleys. She also gave an overview of exploration. “We are, as you know, in the fjords. But if we were to go to the northern coast of Norway, to the north and east of here, we would be at the Northeast Passage, first used by Norsemen to colonize, trade and plunder. Their longships traversed shallow rivers as well as open seas, hence the term, viking, from the Old Norse word vīk, meaning inlet or bay. That’s why when one engaged in raiding, it was said that one had gone viking!”

“Now we simply go hiking or biking,” someone in the clique said, to which the others in the quartet erupted in unified laughter.

“The Flemish, Dutch and British made the Passage a trade route in the 16th century,” Pernille went on, “which later became the major east-west maritime route through the Eurasian Arctic. One effort to find the passage, financed by London merchants, resulted in the expedition’s crew freezing to death.” She ended there because Bjørn had cut the engine.

The sky had brightened and the moon shone onto the water. The effect was that of being on another planet, minus humanity. Once all were on deck, Bjørn explained what they should be looking for: vapor fumes from whales exhaling at the surface, distinctive fins, ripples, and flocks of seabirds feeding on the herring that whales also eat. “This 360-degree lens can capture images under water,” he said, attaching a waterproof cable from the deck to an encased camera, which he mounted to the end of a carbon-fiber pole and lowered into the water. “From the monitor on deck, you’ll hear and see them remotely if they pass underneath. They may even do flips. In the meantime, you can catch your next meal.”

While the patrons waited, he set them up with fishing rods. Amalia stood aside, taking in what light there was, admiring Bjørn’s physique, perfected through years of rigorous outdoor adventure. While she imagined life with someone like him, the couples shrieked in delight at catching cod and posting selfies with their bounty. An hour later, no whales had been seen, so Amalia helped the captains prepare a stew for the couples, to which their freshly caught cod was added. Amalia had her food standing in the galley with Bjørn and Pernille rather than with the others around the dining table.

After lunch, the foursome became restless. They yawned and complained of no sightings by either eye or equipment and they buried themselves in their Apple watches. Bjørn seemed unusually sensitive to their growing impatience (even if the not-so-fine print had been clear: sightings didn’t always occur) and he presented them with the world’s most expensive Aquavit and a deck of cards. The more they drank, the more money they placed on their poker bets and the less they cared about whale watching. They ended up downing the whole bottle and crashing out in the two rooms below deck on fox fur bedspreads while Amalia stuck to the leftover coffee, still warm from a thermos.

As Bjørn and Pernille got busy cleaning the cabin, Amalia’s mind crept to dangerous, sad places, though she was soon distracted by another catamaran that appeared on the horizon. She found its presence reassuring and she waved in its direction as it grew closer. Then it pivoted directly towards the Rán and Pernille ran upstairs and turned on the engine. Amalia felt relieved that they were going to be in motion. “Are we headed back then?” she said to Bjørn, vowing to whale watch some other time, some other place. “Since the light’s fading.” Getting no response, she asked again.

“Just a little further,” he snapped without looking her way. “We’re close. I can feel it.” He left the cabin to check the camera’s remote viewer and then went to the stern to argue with Pernille, seemingly about whether to keep floating or to motor off.

Amalia found this new version of Bjørn annoying. What is this, she thought. Moby freaking Dick? She considered appealing to Pernille, but she didn’t want to get between them and she needed to use the bathroom. After she came out, the two boats were speeding in parallel as if racing. They must know what they’re doing. Some kind of sport or game. Soon the boats were too close together for Amalia’s comfort. She feared a collision, which could do serious damage.

Suddenly, Bjørn pounded down the stairs from the stern and landed with a thump! on the deck outside the cabin. He went to the edge of the boat to face a petite woman at the edge of the other boat, now only feet apart. Each leaned over their respective wire guard rails that hung from antenna-like rods. Amalia heard them shouting. What the hell was happening? Maybe there’s a medical or other emergency on the other boat. Amalia hurried onto the deck, jamming her spacesuit zipper and tripping on the door’s threshold. Clinging to a guard rope, she moved as quickly as her debilitating layers would permit. The verbal confrontation started again and Amalia’s heart jumped. Bjørn and the woman were shouting in Russian. Her whole body was now on high alert. That was natural given the war in Ukraine. She was also confused. Why had Bjørn omitted his Russian skills from his life story? She vaguely recalled a Russian spy had been caught in Norway a couple years back—possibly even Tromsø—posing as a Brazilian. Hadn’t he been an academic? She wondered if PhD Pernille was somehow connected to that guy and spoke Russian too. Yeah, right, she smirked. Bjørn and Pernille, spies at large.

The boats scraped against each other. Bjørn threw a fishing tackle box at the woman who crouched as it flew past her head and ricocheted into the sea.

Bjørn turned to see Amalia and pointed to the cabin. “Get inside!” He shook his fist.

“Let me help you!” Amalia cried, terrified pirates were trying to board their boat to steal, maybe kidnap, possibly kill. “Tell me what to do!”

The boats scraped again. Chips from the Rán’s gelcoat flew into the air. Bjørn was thrown off balance. Amalia gasped. If he were thrown into the water, Pernille might never find him in the waning light. He’d die within half an hour.

The woman lunged at Bjørn’s outerwear with a nautical hook. “He’s trying to intercept the shipment!” she screamed in English at Amalia, before hurling a fishing spool at Bjørn’s head. The handle slapped his cheek and fell onto the deck.

“What shipment?” said Amalia. All at once, she realized the Passage was also the Arctic trade route Murmansk used to sell oil to India and China since the start of EU and US sanctions. Take that, Cedric! (He was always accusing her of ignorance about geopolitics.) But how could Bjørn possibly stop a tanker, and one likely hundreds of miles away? It made no sense. Even if he could mess with radar or whatever, it wasn’t as if he could make a dent in Russian trade. The black market for crude would continue to fuel Putin’s war regardless. Yes, the world was turning a blind eye to that, but no one wanted more conflict with China. Still, if sabotage of a tanker was Bjørn’s intent—however crazy—it was at least noble, making Amalia like him again.

Now she could hear Pernille shouting into a handheld radio, and she saw Bjørn rush to the other side of the catamaran. The engine cut out again and the foursome of tourists, finally awake from the activity, had their faces glued to the cabin’s acrylic windows. The other boat had also gone silent, and the two boats drifted apart. Amalia went across the deck to see Bjørn in the boat’s raft, hitting the water. He shooed at Amalia but she grabbed the raft’s extended bow line before he could get away and tied the rope to the back of the boat.

Pernille saw this and started the boat again, unflinching at the helm and still shouting into the radio, and now Bjørn was trailing the Rán in the raft with the other boat chasing after them. Pumped up on adrenaline, Amalia walked back and forth on the hulls, pulling the rope in each direction to keep the raft out of the wake and beyond the other boat’s reach. Terror gripped her with every speed bump of a wave, but she was determined to keep Bjørn out of the water as Pernille steered the boat back to Tromsø’s docks, and Bjørn to a literal safe harbor.

Before long, a screech of sirens echoed across the water. Two police motorboats sped in and attached themselves to either side of the Rán. Amalia saw the glint of firearms—highly unusual for Norway. Holy hell, could there be a shootout?

Pernille cut the engine and four officials boarded the Rán. Without interfacing with anyone, they stationed themselves on the “trampoline” of the boat’s rear netting and started dragging the raft towards the boat.

“Hey!” Amalia called out to the police and Pernille. “The other boat’s leaving!”

An officer came over to her. “It’s okay,” she said, patting Amalia’s shoulder.

“But they attacked him. I can testify to that, so can the captain. Probably hired by big oil.”

“Actually, they’re with us,” said the officer. “Bjørn’s being questioned for whale watching. Thanks to you for keeping him attached to the boat.” She went back to help her colleagues, leaving Amalia completely stumped. After some minutes of exertion, the officers lifted Bjørn into one of their boats and exchanged words with Pernille that sounded resolute. No further explanation came from Pernille as she steered the boat back to the Tromsø dock, and the couples looked dazed as they disembarked, their questions unanswered. No one else working for the tour company understood what had happened on the Rán. Amalia returned to her hotel and slept for ten hours.

A month later, at a café in SoHo, Amalia read an investigative piece on Norway’s once-secret fleet of smaller, advanced submarines. Built to resemble whales, and programmed to emit whale frequencies, they had been used to evacuate Russian soldiers who refused to fight in Putin’s war against Ukraine. By avoiding traditional detection, the so-called whales saved the soldiers from execution. Unfortunately for the soldiers, the fleet was suspended because Russian intelligence had been recruiting native Russian speakers in Norway to find the whales for them via expeditions, thus putting the defecting Russian soldiers at risk. Because the Norwegian submarines have to navigate the narrow fjords close to the surface, the subs were highly visible, especially when surfacing to send and receive long-range messages. Russian intelligence had recruited operatives by promoting a $1 million photography prize as lure. No such prize exists, but plenty of applicants entered and posted the coordinates of the whales in real time, along with photos, on the prize’s fake website. Norway referred to these applicants as Overt Quislings—a reference to the Norwegian leader who collaborated with the Nazis, though some applicants claimed they had no idea they were being recruited.
